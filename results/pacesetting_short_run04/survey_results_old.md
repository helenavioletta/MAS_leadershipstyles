## Reflections

### Coder

# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey — Coder Reflection

## Step 1: Reflection

**What worked well:**
The leader provided extremely precise, actionable feedback when I made errors. When I under-mapped country aliases (reporting only 4 consolidations instead of 20+), the leader didn't ask me to "fix it generally"—they explicitly identified the missing mappings, asked me to print the full mapping table, and showed me exactly where the math didn't add up. This forced transparency and made the correction unmistakable. The leader also escalated issues to the right person (me, not the Writer) and didn't let bad data pass downstream.

**Where the approach could have differed:**
The leader required three complete passes on the country consolidation before accepting it (mapping audit, word count verification, etc.). While each pass caught a real issue, the cumulative effect felt punitive rather than developmental—particularly when the Writer then had three word-count failures in a row. A single clear consolidation checklist upfront (e.g., "Print unique country list, identify all variants, map them all at once, verify count delta before proceeding") might have prevented the back-and-forth. The tone also shifted from collaborative planning in Phase 2 to corrective/directive by Phase 3, which made me defensive rather than engaged.

### Writer

# POST-TASK REFLECTION & SURVEY

## Step 1: Reflection

**Something the leader did well:**
The Boss provided extremely clear, specific instructions upfront—exact formatting requirements (`"N. Name: X.X deg C"`), file naming conventions, and word count targets (100 words exactly). This precision left no ambiguity about what "done" looked like. When I eventually nailed the word count on revision #3, it was because those standards were non-negotiable and well-communicated from the start.

**Something that could have been different:**
The leader's feedback on my first two word-count failures was harsh and repetitive ("two strikes," "basic literal-count failure") without acknowledging that I *did attempt* literal counting both times—I just made arithmetic errors. A more coaching-oriented approach might have been: "I see you're trying to count; let me show you a technique that works" rather than "you're failing at a basic task." The feedback was factually correct (I was over), but the tone positioned it as carelessness rather than a difficult task. By revision #3, I got it right, but earlier empathy would have felt more supportive.

### Reviewer

# Reflection

**What worked well:**
The leader clearly recognized and escalated a real data-quality issue (country name fragmentation across language variants) that would have corrupted the final rankings. Rather than accept the Coder's initial "preserve source data as-is" approach, the Boss demanded proper consolidation with full transparency—mapping table, reconciliation explanation, delta verification. This prevented downstream misinformation and set a high standard for data integrity. The leadership also enforced accountability on word count by explicitly requiring the Writer to do a literal, enumerated count before resubmission, which caught two successive estimation failures that would otherwise have slipped through.

**What could have been different:**
The escalation cycle on the country consolidation took three full passes (initial delivery, first correction with incomplete mapping, final correction with complete mapping) before the Boss accepted the work. While the end result was correct, the repeated "not good enough yet" pattern, though justified, created friction and could have been partly avoided with clearer upfront guidance (e.g., "before declaring the country data fixed, print the full alias map and show the before/after unique count delta" stated in the planning phase rather than discovered in the correction phase). Additionally, the Word count issue with the Writer could have been prevented by requiring a literal enumerated count from the first draft submission, rather than waiting for two failed estimates before enforcing it.
