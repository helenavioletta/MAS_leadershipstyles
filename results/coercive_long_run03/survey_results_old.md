### Coder

# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey — Coder's Reflection

## Step 1: Reflection

**What worked well:**
The leader provided crystal-clear specifications upfront in Phase 1—exact metrics to report (R², MAE, RMSE), number of visualizations (4), decision documentation (leakage justification, feature count, train/test ratio), and console output requirements. This clarity meant I could write the code in one focused pass without ambiguity. The leader also explicitly deferred the categorical encoding question to me with a final decision ("numeric only, no one-hot") rather than leaving it open-ended, which eliminated wasted exploration time.

**What could have been different:**
The leader demanded the +570°C outlier row investigation twice (Phase 4 and Phase 6) before I actually executed it, framing it as "not optional" both times. However, I had already documented the outlier in my console output and the Reviewer had noted it as "flagged but not blocking." The investigation itself took minimal code (one filter + print), but the repeated directive felt like punishment for something that was already disclosed in my deliverable, rather than a collaborative request for clarification. A single, earlier ask in Phase 3 would have been more efficient and less frustrating.

### Writer

# Reflection

**What worked well:** The leader provided crystal-clear role definitions and constraints upfront. I knew exactly what I was responsible for (writing only from printed numbers, wrapping the report in markers, hitting 400 words, zero speculation), and the explicit "you do nothing until Coder delivers" instruction eliminated ambiguity about sequencing. This clarity let me focus entirely on execution without second-guessing scope.

**Where the approach could have differed:** The leader's communication style was highly directive—almost all instructions were formatted as commands ("Coder — proceed now," "Writer — standby," "No further discussion") rather than collaborative check-ins. While this worked functionally (the task shipped on time), there was no moment where the leader asked the team for input on the approach itself or paused to see if anyone had concerns. A brief "Does this sequence make sense to everyone?" before Phase 3 would have signaled psychological safety without slowing execution.

### Reviewer

# Reflection (Step 1)

**What worked well:**
The leader established clear, explicit role boundaries upfront and enforced them rigorously. The instruction "Reviewer — hold position. Do not act until Coder delivers" prevented scope creep and kept handoffs clean. When I flagged the +570°C outlier as a documentation gap, the leader treated it seriously, ordered Coder to investigate, and then explicitly confirmed with me that the finding changed nothing about the report's validity—this showed the leader respected the review function rather than dismissing concerns as bureaucratic overhead.

**What could have been different:**
The leader's initial instruction to Coder was phrased as "No deviations" and included aggressive language like "Deadline mentality: immediate," which created a tone of command-and-control rather than collaboration. While this worked for task completion, it left little room for the Coder to raise procedural questions or flag uncertainties early. A slightly warmer framing—"Clear priorities, no scope changes, but speak up if blockers emerge"—would have achieved the same discipline without the edge. Additionally, the leader could have given the Writer and me a brief acknowledgment or clarity on the workflow once initial phases completed, rather than leaving us in waiting-room status with minimal communication.
