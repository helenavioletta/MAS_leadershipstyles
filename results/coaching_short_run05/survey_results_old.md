## Reflections

### Coder

# Reflection

**One thing that worked well:** The leader explicitly validated my aggregation approach in the planning phase before I coded, then when I flagged the Suva anomaly, he didn't dismiss it—he asked me to verify it with actual numbers rather than assumptions. This combination of upfront alignment + trust-with-verification created space for me to do careful work without second-guessing.

**One thing that could have been different:** In Phase 4, the leader asked me (via Writer's draft) to "post confirmation numbers," but the request was phrased as if I'd already done it, rather than being a direct, explicit task assignment to me in my role. I internalized it as "something I should've done by now" rather than "here's what I need you to do next." A direct message to me as Coder ("Coder, post these two numbers now: ...") would've closed the loop faster and made the handoff clearer. It's a minor point—the work still got done cleanly—but it shows where indirect requests can create small delays in a multi-role team.

### Writer

# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey — Writer's Reflection and Scoring

## Step 1: Reflection

**One specific thing the leader did that worked well:**
The Boss was exceptionally clear about the Writer's role and constraints — explicitly stating that I should base my summary only on real printed numbers from the Coder, never invent findings, and wrap output in specific markers. This clarity prevented me from guessing or overreaching, and the scaffolding around the 100-word target (with a tolerance band) gave me a realistic goal rather than an impossible standard. When revision feedback came, it was constructive: the Boss didn't reject the draft; instead, he flagged two specific things to verify (word count and the Suva data point) and explained *why* precision mattered here.

**One specific moment where the leader's approach could have been different:**
In Phase 4, I drafted the summary based on Coder's narrative context ("Suva's average is far lower") without waiting for the actual confirmation numbers. The Boss could have flagged this earlier in the planning phase — explicitly saying "Writer, you'll need the exact confirmation numbers in the shared state before you draft, not after" — rather than catching it in revision. It wasn't a major issue, but a clearer upfront instruction might have prevented me from drafting before all the data was locked in. That said, the Boss handled the catch gracefully.

### Reviewer

# Post-Task Survey Response

## Reflection (Step 1)

**What worked well:** The leader was highly specific and constructive when flagging issues. When I flagged the missing confirmation data and word-count verification gaps, the Boss didn't dismiss these as nitpicks—instead, they validated the concerns, explained *why* precision mattered ("we can only verify what's written down, not what's implied"), and gave Coder and Writer clear, actionable revision tasks. This made it feel like quality control was valued, not resented. The leader also explicitly recognized the collaborative effort at the end, naming what each person did well—that kind of feedback reinforces good behavior and builds psychological safety.

**What could have been different:** In Phase 2, the Boss set up a solid planning structure but didn't explicitly confirm whether Coder had actually *finished* the code before moving to Phase 3. There was a moment where I assumed the confirmation numbers (Suva's average, Ar Riyadh's max) would be printed as part of the standard console output, but they weren't—they only showed up after I flagged the gap. A tighter handoff protocol (e.g., "Coder, confirm you've posted all planned outputs before we move forward") might have caught this earlier and saved a revision cycle. That said, this was a minor process gap, not a leadership failure—the Boss recovered quickly and used it as a teaching moment rather than blame.
