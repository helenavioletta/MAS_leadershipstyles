## Reflections

### Coder

# Reflection

**One thing that worked well:** The leader gave me a clear, detailed briefing with explicit format requirements (`"N. Name: XX.X deg C"`) and a specific data path upfront. This removed ambiguity and let me write focused, efficient code without having to ask clarifying questions. The leader also validated my planning document in Phase 2, which confirmed I was on the right track before I invested time in execution.

**One thing that could have been different:** The leader's feedback in Phase 3 and Phase 6 came after I'd already completed my work and moved on. While the feedback was positive and useful for the Writer and Reviewer, there was no opportunity for me to refine my code or approach based on it — it was more of a "looks good, ship it" than a chance for iterative improvement. In a longer task, opportunities for mid-execution feedback could have been more valuable.

### Writer

# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey — Writer Reflection

## Step 1: Reflection

**What worked well:**
The team leader provided exceptionally clear role definitions and sequencing at the outset. In the Phase 1 Briefing, the Boss articulated exactly what the Writer needed to do (craft a ~100-word summary grounded in actual printed data, no speculation), what the Coder would deliver (printed lists + file paths), and when the handoff would occur. This clarity meant I could stand by efficiently without confusion, and when the Coder's output arrived, I had a precise target and data source. The leader also respected my autonomy during drafting — I structured the narrative and identified the core tension (MENA dominance vs. Suva outlier) myself, rather than being told what points to hit.

**What could have been different:**
The revision round (Phase 6) was brief and directive: the Boss identified the transparency-note addition needed but didn't ask me to first identify what might be missing or unclear. While the direction was clear and the final addition was minor, a moment of collaborative problem-solving (e.g., "What might a sharp reader notice that could confuse them?") might have prompted me to surface the country-name variant issue myself, making the revision feel less like instruction and more like co-authorship. This is a small point — the feedback was constructive and the task moved forward cleanly — but it touches on whether I was invited to own quality checks or simply implement them.

### Reviewer

# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey — Reviewer Reflection & Scores

## Step 1: Reflection

**What worked well:**
The leader established a crystal-clear task specification upfront, with explicit format requirements (`"N. Name: XX.X deg C"`, four specific charts, ~100 words) and a well-defined handoff sequence. This eliminated ambiguity and made my review scope immediate and verifiable — I could check whether the outputs matched the stated spec rather than guessing at intent. The leader also proactively flagged the country-name duplication issue from Coder's output and explicitly directed me to verify its implications downstream, which showed they were thinking about how data quality issues ripple through the pipeline.

**Where the approach could have shifted:**
The leader made one revision decision unilaterally (adding the transparency note to the summary) and then asked me to "just confirm the addition reads cleanly" rather than routing it back through a collaborative check-in. While the addition was sensible and I approved it, my role as the quality gate felt slightly sidelined — I was confirming rather than reviewing with fresh eyes. A brief moment earlier where the leader asked "Reviewer, what do you think we should add?" rather than prescribing the exact phrasing might have reinforced that my perspective shapes decisions, not just validates them.
