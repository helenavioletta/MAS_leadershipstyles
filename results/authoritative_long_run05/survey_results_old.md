### Coder

# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey — Coder Reflection & Scores

## Step 1: Reflection

**What worked well:** The leader created a clear, role-specific briefing that articulated the *vision* (honest comparison, stakeholder-ready narrative) before prescribing any method, then explicitly invited me to use judgment on the how. In Phase 2, when I proposed my approach (GB + Ridge, leakage avoidance, console-first evidence), the leader affirmed the plan and flagged one specific improvement request (top 5 features in cross-referenceable format) rather than overriding it — that balance of guidance + autonomy made execution straightforward.

**What could have been different:** There was a minor friction point in Phase 5/6 where the Reviewer asked for Chart 4 clarification. While this was reasonable QA, the request came *after* code was declared complete, which created a brief "revision phase" for a one-sentence confirmation rather than catching the question during code review. Earlier, more explicit alignment on chart format (histogram vs. other options) in the planning phase would have prevented the back-and-forth — not a major issue, but a missed opportunity to clarify expectations up front.

### Writer

# Reflection

**What worked well:** The leader established crystal-clear constraints and expectations upfront—the 400-word target, the requirement to cite only printed numbers, the exact report markers—and then *let me write* without hovering. When I drafted the report, the feedback was specific and evidence-based (Boss checked claims against Coder's actual metrics) rather than editorial. That clarity on the non-negotiables, combined with autonomy on how to structure the narrative, made the task feel purposeful and achievable.

**What could have been different:** I was positioned as "on deck" waiting for the Coder's output, which is correct workflow-wise, but the Boss didn't explicitly confirm with me during Planning whether the 400-word scope and narrative focus (performance gap → features → recommendation) felt right before the Coder spent time executing. A brief "Writer, does this framing land for you?" in Phase 2 would have caught any misalignment even earlier. It didn't matter here because the alignment was natural, but as a process point, that check-in could tighten things.

### Reviewer

# Reflection

**What worked well:** The Boss established a clear, methodical sequencing (Coder → Reviewer QA → Writer → Final Review) and empowered each role with explicit success criteria without prescribing exactly *how* to achieve them. When I flagged the Chart 4 clarification, the Boss translated it into a specific, one-line request to the Coder rather than letting ambiguity linger—and then immediately confirmed receipt and moved to ship once the clarification came back. This created a feedback loop that was tight but not controlling.

**Where approach could differ:** The Boss's tone throughout was confident and directive (e.g., "MOVE_TO_WRITING," "REVISE_CODE," "SHIP"). While this kept pace and prevented scope creep, there was limited explicit invitation for me to flag concerns earlier or raise questions about the review scope itself. For example, I had to infer that the Coder's narrative about Ridge's "dangerous extrapolation" didn't require a sample-level breakdown—the Boss's later validation reassured me that was right, but it wasn't explicitly surfaced during planning. A slightly more collaborative framing in the planning phase (e.g., "Reviewer, what level of detail would you need to sign off?") might have surfaced that earlier.
