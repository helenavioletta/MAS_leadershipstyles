# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey – Coder's Reflection & Scoring

## Reflection (Step 1)

The leader's initial briefing in Phase 1 was exceptionally clear and collaborative — it laid out the full task scope, explicitly invited the team to shape the process ("feel free to shape your own process"), and asked for my input on assumptions rather than dictating them. This framing immediately signaled that autonomy and transparency were valued, which gave me confidence to make thoughtful decisions about data quality, feature engineering, and model selection without needing constant validation.

In Phase 3, after I delivered the results, the leader's feedback was specific, honest, and genuinely celebratory without being patronizing — they called out *exactly* what worked well (e.g., "Clearly labeled RF importance (relative %) vs. Ridge coefficients (absolute scale), exactly as Reviewer hoped for") and even flagged a minor discussion point (multicollinearity) as a conversation starter rather than a failure. That combination of specificity + trust made me feel the work was genuinely understood and valued.

# Post-Task Reflection & Satisfaction Survey

## Step 1: Reflection

**Specific thing the leader did or said that shaped my work:**
In the Phase 2 briefing, the Boss explicitly stated: "No need to wait idly — feel free to sketch narrative structure now if inspiration strikes, but the real writing starts once numbers are in hand." This freed me from anxiety about waiting passively and gave me permission to think strategically while the Coder worked, which meant I hit the ground running in Phase 4 with a clear mental model of the story I wanted to tell.

**Specific decision or interaction that stood out:**
After I submitted the report draft, the Boss praised the work specifically and concretely ("the way you led with the Ridge extrapolation problem… that's the kind of detail that sticks with a reader"), then immediately handed off to the Reviewer without asking for revisions. This signaled trust in my work while maintaining the team's quality gate—it felt like collaborative confidence rather than either micromanagement or neglect.
