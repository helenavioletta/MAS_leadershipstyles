## Reflections

### Coder

# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey — Coder's Response

## Step 1: Reflection

**What worked well:**
The leader explicitly invited input during Phase 2 planning ("I want everyone's input," "does this plan work for you, or do you want to adjust") and then *incorporated* our concerns into the final decisions. When Reviewer flagged the temporal/spatial leakage risk, Boss didn't dismiss it—he acknowledged it, made a call (stick with random 80/20 but document it), and gave me clear instructions on *what to print* so the limitation was transparent. That kind of collaborative lock-in before execution meant I wasn't second-guessing myself mid-code.

**What could have been different:**
In Phase 3, once I'd completed the work and posted my summary, there was a moment where Boss said "nice work" but then immediately asked me to double-check something specific for the Reviewer's upcoming audit. This wasn't unreasonable—it was actually appropriate diligence—but it felt like he was reviewing my output *as* I was submitting it, rather than letting me know upfront if there were edge cases he wanted explicitly verified. A lighter touch during the hand-off to Reviewer might have felt more like "you're done, Reviewer audits" versus "you're done, but also confirm these specifics for the audit."

### Writer

# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey — Writer Reflection & Scores

## Step 1: Reflection

**What worked well:**
The Boss established clear role definitions and invited input from all three team members before locking in the approach. Rather than dictating the task structure, the Boss asked the Coder about data quality concerns, the Reviewer about audit risks, and me about what console outputs would make writing easier — then incorporated all of that into the final plan. This meant my job as Writer was set up for success: I received a formatted metrics table, clearly labeled feature lists, explicit exclusion rationale, and the leakage caveat all printed to console, exactly as I'd requested.

**What could have been different:**
When Reviewer caught the residual direction error in my first draft, the correction came through the Boss rather than directly from Reviewer. It was still precise and actionable, but a more direct feedback loop (Reviewer → Writer, with Boss looping in for visibility) might have been slightly faster and preserved the collaborative feel. This is a minor point — the outcome was correct and the process still worked — but it's worth noting.

### Reviewer

# Post-Task Reflection & Survey Response

## Step 1: Reflection

**What worked well:** The Boss explicitly solicited Reviewer input during Phase 2 planning ("Reviewer — what should we watch out for upfront?"), then incorporated those concerns directly into the execution plan — the temporal/spatial leakage caveat, standardization scope clarification, and visualization data requirements all came from my flagging, and the Boss locked them in as formal decisions rather than dismissing them. This meant when I did my final audit, I was checking against criteria I'd helped define, which made the review more precise and gave me genuine ownership of the quality gate.

**What could have been different:** In Phase 6, after I flagged the residual-direction error, the Boss immediately drafted the exact correction for Writer rather than letting me confirm the fix independently. While the correction was correct, it slightly undermined the Reviewer role — I should have been the one to propose the precise wording or at least verify the Boss's suggested fix before it went to Writer. It felt like the Boss was doing the final editorial check, not me.
