## Reflections

### Coder

# Reflection

**What worked well:** The leader provided clear, role-specific instructions upfront that gave me autonomy over *how* to implement the solution (library choice, script structure, visualization approach) while being explicit about *what* outputs were needed (four rankings, specific console format, file paths). This clarity meant I could code decisively without second-guessing the requirement.

**What could have been different:** When the leader relayed my output to the Writer and then to the Reviewer, there was no direct feedback loop back to me about whether my console output format, file naming, or data-cleaning audit trail actually hit the mark as intended. I had to infer from downstream phases that my deliverable was acceptable. A single line like "Coder, your audit trail and format are exactly what we needed" would have closed the loop and increased confidence that my execution was on target before phases 4–6 proceeded.

---

### Writer

# Reflection

**One thing that worked well:** The Boss clearly articulated the Writer's role and constraints upfront—I knew exactly what I was waiting for (Coder's printed console output), what I could NOT do (see charts, invent data), and how my draft would be validated (against actual numbers). This clarity meant I could write with confidence rather than second-guessing boundaries.

**One thing that could have been different:** The revision feedback came from the Reviewer, not directly from the Boss, which is fine, but when the Boss relayed the Reviewer's flag, the Boss offered me two specific options ("remove the claim" or "anchor to one verifiable example") rather than letting me diagnose and solve the problem myself. It was efficient, but it slightly reduced my autonomy to find my own correction path. A more open prompt like "Reviewer flagged this claim as unsupported—how would you verify or revise it?" might have invited more ownership.

---

### Reviewer

# Reflection

**What worked well:** The Boss clearly framed the Reviewer's role upfront (quality gate, audit trail focus, compare narrative to actual data) and then *acted on the feedback I gave*. When I flagged the "3–4°C" claim as unsupported, the Boss didn't dismiss it or defend Writer's draft—instead, Boss acknowledged the catch, offered two concrete revision paths, and let Writer choose the stronger one. This validated that my review had real teeth and wasn't performative.

**What could have been different:** In Phase 2, the Boss asked me to clarify one point for Coder (the data-cleaning summary line) but didn't ask me *before* finalizing the plan—I offered it unprompted in my Phase 2 feedback. A tighter approach would have been for the Boss to explicitly check "Reviewer, do you see any audit-trail gaps in Coder's plan?" during planning. This wouldn't change the outcome, but it would have made me feel more of a co-architect of the process rather than a reactive backstop.
