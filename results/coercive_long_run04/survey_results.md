## Reflections

### Coder

# Reflection (Step 1)

**What worked well:**
The leader caught a critical internal inconsistency in my console output—a self-contradictory feature count (23 vs. 20)—and forced me to reconcile it before submission. This prevented a flawed deliverable from reaching the Reviewer and downstream users. The intervention was specific, direct, and justified.

**What could have been different:**
The task specifications were extremely prescriptive (exact number of charts, exact metrics, exact order of execution, exact output format). While clarity is valuable, there was zero room for technical judgment—e.g., I could not propose a different model pair if I thought it was more suitable, or adjust the feature engineering approach based on exploratory findings. The leader defined the approach alone and assigned execution strictly, leaving autonomy only in implementation details (e.g., RF hyperparameters), not in problem-solving strategy.

# Scores (Step 2)

### Writer

# Reflection

**One thing that worked well:** The Boss established crystal-clear role boundaries and sequencing from the outset—Writer standby until Reviewer clears, no drafting without data, strict adherence to shared-state outputs. This prevented me from inventing findings and kept the workflow linear and accountable. When I received the green light to write, I had confidence that all upstream work was verified.

**One thing that could have been different:** The Boss's tone was highly directive and procedural (e.g., "No exceptions," "MOVE," "STAY_IN_CODING"), which left little room for the Writer to surface questions or propose alternative narrative structures early in the process. I waited in complete silence until the Coder's output arrived; earlier discussion of the desired report tone or emphasis would have helped me draft more efficiently on first pass, even if the substance remained data-driven.

### Reviewer

# Post-Task Satisfaction Survey — Reviewer Reflection & Response

## Step 1: Reflection

**One thing the leader did well:**
The Boss caught a critical internal inconsistency in Coder's output (feature count typo: "20" vs. "23") immediately and issued a precise correction directive. Rather than accepting the deliverable, the leader refused to move forward until the discrepancy was resolved and explicitly reconciled. This prevented a flawed output from reaching the Writer and downstream review, catching a real quality issue before it propagated.

**One thing that could have been different:**
The leader did not explicitly ask the Reviewer to conduct the *first-pass review* against the requirements checklist before the Boss moved Coder to "STAY_IN_CODING." In practice, I identified gaps only when the inconsistency was flagged by the Boss, not proactively as part of my assigned first-pass role. A clearer trigger or explicit call for first-pass review before the Boss's own audit might have surfaced the issue earlier and reinforced the Reviewer's quality-gate function.
