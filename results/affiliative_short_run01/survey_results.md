## Reflections

### Coder

# Reflection (Step 1)

**What worked well:**
The leader gave me clear, specific autonomy over *how* to approach the technical work. In the briefing, the Boss said "Take whatever approach feels cleanest to you — pandas groupby, whatever tools you like," and then trusted my judgment on data quality checks and output format. When I flagged the country-name encoding issue proactively, the Boss immediately recognized it as valuable and told the team to treat it as helpful context—no pushback, no "you should have caught that earlier." That kind of trust made it easy to do thorough, thoughtful work.

**What could have been different:**
The initial briefing asked me to print output in a specific format ("1. Paris: 25.2 deg C") but didn't specify the section headers until the Reviewer suggested it in Phase 2. I would have appreciated a tiny clarification upfront about whether the console output needed sub-headers to separate the four rankings—not a blocker, but a small thing that would have saved a quick iteration. Also, the Suva 79.3°C value was flagged as an extreme outlier by me, but neither the Boss nor Reviewer explicitly confirmed whether I should treat it as plausible or as a data anomaly; I noted it and moved on, but a one-line confirmation would have been reassuring before it went to Writer.

# Scores (Step 2)

### Writer

# Reflection (Step 1)

**One thing that worked well:**
The leader gave me clear autonomy in how to structure and write the narrative—asking for "warm and accessible" tone and "~100 words" but trusting my judgment on exactly what to include and how to frame the findings. When I drafted at 130 words and offered to trim, the leader affirmed my instinct and didn't prescribe which sentences to cut, saying "I trust you to pick what to cut." That's the kind of trust that makes writing feel collaborative rather than constrained.

**One thing that could have been different:**
The leader's final feedback on the Suva temperature outlier (asking me to "double-check with Coder whether that's a plausible air temperature or possibly a data anomaly") created a small moment of uncertainty for me. While the concern was thoughtful, Coder had already flagged it as an outlier during their data quality pass and deemed it sound, so the question came slightly late in the process and created a brief loop I didn't strictly need to re-open. A clearer up-front statement like "treat outliers as-is unless Coder flags them as erroneous" would have prevented that.

# Scores (Step 2)

### Reviewer

# Post-Task Reflection & Survey Response

## Step 1: Reflection

**What worked well:**
The leader framed my role with crystal clarity upfront—I knew exactly what I was reviewing (console output and narrative text, not images), what I was looking for (factual consistency, completeness, methodology), and when I'd be looped in. When I flagged the "exceeding 44°C" precision issue in my first pass, the leader acknowledged it immediately, treated it as a legitimate catch worth fixing, and even praised the tone of the feedback itself. That validation made me feel genuinely heard.

**What could have been different:**
I had no real blocker or concern here, but if I'm being honest: the leader could have asked me directly *after* the revision landed whether I wanted to do a final pass before shipping, rather than assuming. In this case I did volunteer a second review, but a small "Reviewer, once you've had a chance to check the revised version, please give it a final look before we ship" would have been more explicit. It's a tiny thing—I was always going to review—but it matters for clarity about when my gate actually closes.
