## Reflections

### Coder

# Reflection (Step 1)

**What worked well:**
The team leader created a collaborative planning phase (Phase 2) where each role (Coder, Writer, Reviewer) had explicit space to voice concerns and input before work started. This meant I wasn't handed a rigid spec—I got to flag the duplicate-city-name issue upfront and propose how to handle it, which built confidence in my technical approach. When I later discovered the country-name normalization issue, the leader didn't dismiss it; instead, they asked clarifying diagnostic questions and trusted me to report the impact transparently before making a decision. That combination of early buy-in plus trust during execution was very effective.

**Where the approach could have been different:**
In Phase 3, after I reported the country-name inconsistency, the leader pivoted me back into coding ("STAY_IN_CODING") without a full pause to let me weigh in on whether normalization was the right call. I had recommended Option A, but the leader made the call unilaterally and immediately sent me back to code. It felt like the collaborative tone from Phase 2 shifted to a more directive mode once execution started. A brief "Does this direction work for you?" before the pivot would have maintained the collaborative feel.

# Scores (Step 2)

### Writer

# Reflection

**What worked well:**
The leader (Boss) used a structured, transparent approach to task planning—soliciting input from all three team members before locking in assignments, rather than dictating a plan outright. When I raised concerns about needing actual printed numbers rather than chart descriptions, the Boss acknowledged that as a legitimate clarification and ensured the Coder understood to provide console output for me to work from. This made it clear I had agency in defining what I needed to succeed.

**Where it could have been different:**
During the revision phase, the Reviewer's feedback on the country-ranking framing was accurate and necessary, but the Boss's follow-up instructions were very prescriptive—telling me to "name more countries explicitly," "acknowledge the Gulf/Southeast Asia split," and "fix the closing line"—rather than asking me to rethink the framing and present a solution. I complied successfully, but it felt more like implementing a directive than being invited to problem-solve. A more open framing (e.g., "The country paragraph doesn't capture the post-normalization story—how would you revise it?") would have preserved more autonomy in how I addressed the issue.

### Reviewer

# Reflection

**What worked well:**
The Boss demonstrated excellent judgment when confronted with Coder's discovery of country-name inconsistencies. Rather than dismissing it as a minor cosmetic issue, the leader immediately recognized it as a data integrity problem that would distort the rankings and asked for a diagnostic before deciding on a fix. When I flagged the Writer's incomplete framing of the country rankings post-normalization, the Boss validated my concern and gave the Writer clear, specific revision guidance that addressed exactly what was wrong—not vague notes, but actionable changes tied to the actual data story.

**Where approach could differ:**
The Boss used a somewhat directive tone when sending the Writer back for revision ("Fix required" language), though the actual guidance was sound. A slightly more collaborative framing—e.g., asking the Writer to reflect on why the country paragraph needed adjustment before prescribing exact changes—might have deepened the Writer's understanding of the data quality issue and made future reviews easier. That said, given time constraints and the need for clarity, the directive approach was pragmatic and the feedback was ultimately absorbed well.
