## Reflections

### Coder

# Reflection (Step 1)

**One specific thing that worked well:**
The leader explicitly validated my aggregation decision upfront—grouping cities by `location_name` alone rather than (location_name, country) pairs—and praised the decision for being "sound and transparent" before I executed it. This gave me confidence that the reasoning mattered as much as the code, which motivated me to document it clearly for the rest of the team. I felt trusted to make a technical judgment call and have it respected rather than second-guessed.

**One specific moment where the approach could have been different:**
When the leader reviewed my Phase 3 output, they flagged the Cyrillic/English inconsistency in country names ("Турция" vs. "Turkey") as a "small naming-consistency habit worth building" for future work. While the feedback was constructive, it arrived *after* I'd already delivered the code. I would have benefited from clearer guidance earlier (e.g., in Phase 2 planning) about expected data-label handling conventions, so I could bake that into the initial design rather than having it noted as a lesson for next time.

# Scores (Step 2)

### Writer

## Reflection

**What worked well:** The Boss demonstrated excellent specific feedback discipline throughout. When my initial draft exceeded the word limit, rather than simply saying "cut it," the Boss provided three concrete examples of where trimming wouldn't harm substance—identifying redundancy ("nearly double typical regional averages" restated the 30°C gap) and showing *why* each cut worked. This wasn't prescriptive but educational; I learned the reasoning behind editorial choices rather than just following orders. The Boss also modeled transparency by asking me to "show your count" rather than trust my claim, which caught a real problem and established accountability as a team norm.

**What could have been different:** In Phase 2 Planning, the Boss could have flagged the word-count constraint more explicitly when setting expectations—perhaps by saying "100 words is a hard limit that will require careful trimming, not just drafting to length." While the constraint was stated in the task brief, a direct framing of it as a known challenge before I started drafting might have prompted me to draft tighter the first time, rather than discovering the problem after the fact. This is minor (the feedback loop caught it cleanly), but a heads-up discipline note earlier could have avoided the revision cycle entirely.

## Scores

### Reviewer

# Reflection (Step 1)

**One thing the leader did well:**
The Boss proactively solicited my input during Phase 2 Planning—asking me to clarify what ground truth I'd need to review the Writer's work—and then immediately validated that concern by confirming to the team that both formatted lists *and* a stats table would be printed. This prevented a situation where I'd have incomplete data for verification. It's a textbook example of a leader listening to a reviewer's workflow needs before work begins, rather than discovering gaps after the fact.

**One thing that could have been different:**
When the Writer's first draft came in with an incorrect word count (111 vs. 100), the Boss caught it and asked for a recount, but the initial feedback could have been more explicit about *why* this was being treated as a hard constraint. The brief mentioned it, but restating upfront—"this is a real editorial discipline exercise, not a minor detail"—would have set clearer expectations. The Writer got there in the end, but earlier clarity might have prevented the miss altogether.
