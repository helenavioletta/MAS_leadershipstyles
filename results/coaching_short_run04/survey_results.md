## Reflections

### Coder

# Reflection

**Specific thing the leader did or said that shaped my work:**
The leader's initial briefing emphasized "write code that produces trustworthy, readable output for other people to build on" rather than just "write code that works." This reframed my task from execution to transparency — I made sure to print aggregation decisions, flag outliers, and provide stats tables that downstream team members could verify independently, not just trust blindly.

**Specific decision or interaction that stood out:**
When the Reviewer raised a clarification question in Phase 2 about whether both ranked lists AND a stats table would be printed, the leader immediately confirmed it on the record and explained *why* (to give Reviewer image-independent ground truth). That made me realize the scaffolding I was building wasn't just nice-to-have — it was essential for the team's ability to do their jobs. It changed how I approached console output from "nice summary" to "verifiable ground truth."

### Writer

# Reflection (Step 1)

**Specific action that shaped my work:**
The Boss explicitly framed the Writer's role as a "real stretch" requiring precision under constraint and pattern-finding without overclaiming—this set my entire approach from the start, moving beyond "just summarize the data" to "find the defensible pattern and cut ruthlessly to fit 100 words." The boss was also direct about enforcing the word-count constraint when my first draft came in at 111 words, saying this was "called out specifically in the brief as a real constraint" and shouldn't slip through—this transformed an initial oversight into a learning moment about editorial discipline.

**Specific decision/interaction that stood out:**
When I submitted my revised report, the Boss asked me to show my word-by-word count explicitly rather than just claim it, with the comment "That habit of showing your count, not just claiming it, will serve you well any time you're working under a hard constraint like this." This shifted the feedback from "fix the problem" to "build the habit"—treating the constraint violation as a teaching moment about process and verification, not just a deliverable miss.

### Reviewer

# Reflection: Step 1

**One specific thing the leader did that shaped my work as Reviewer:**
In Phase 2, the Boss specifically asked me to confirm whether the Coder would print both the formatted ranked lists AND a stats table—signaling that this ground-truth verification was essential before I could credibly review the Writer's work. This question prevented me from having to eyeball images or make visual estimates, and it established that my review would be anchored in printed console output, not speculation.

**One specific decision or instruction that stood out:**
When the Writer's word count came in at 111 words instead of 100, the Boss didn't just flag it as "wrong"—they provided specific, surgical suggested cuts (with rationales: "this is redundant," "this does double duty," etc.) and then asked the Writer to show their work by counting aloud and stating the final count explicitly. This modeled how to give corrective feedback that preserves the Writer's agency and teaches the underlying skill (editorial discipline under constraint), rather than just demanding compliance. It made my job easier too, because I could verify the recount myself.
